\chapter{State of the Art}
\label{chap:state_of_art}

\section{Artificial Intelligence in Clinical Prediction}

\Gls{ai} and \gls{ml} have been applied to a wide range of medical prediction tasks, including disease detection, prognosis, hospital readmission, and risk stratification. Their adoption has been supported by the increasing availability of electronic health records, public clinical datasets, and software libraries for statistical learning. In tabular clinical applications, commonly used model families include logistic regression, \glspl{dt}, \glspl{rf}, \glspl{svm}, nearest-neighbour methods, boosting algorithms, and neural networks \citep{ahmed_artificial_2022, bharati_review_2024}.

The relationship between model complexity and interpretability is not absolute. Logistic regression coefficients can often be inspected directly, while individual \glspl{dt} expose explicit decision paths. Conversely, ensembles, kernel methods, and neural networks are generally more difficult to interpret without additional tools. Nevertheless, an intrinsically interpretable model is not automatically clinically valid, and a complex model is not automatically more accurate. Reliability depends on the data-generating process, preprocessing, validation design, \gls{calibration}, discrimination, and intended use rather than on model family alone \citep{barredo_arrieta_explainable_2020}.

In clinical contexts, predictions are commonly expected to support rather than replace professional judgement. Explanations can help users inspect whether a fitted model relies on plausible variables, identify unexpected dependencies, and investigate individual predictions. They do not, however, convert statistical associations into causal mechanisms or establish that a system is safe for clinical deployment \citep{tjoa_survey_2021, yang_unbox_2021}. This distinction is fundamental to the interpretation of post-hoc \gls{xai} results.

\section{Reproducibility in Medical Machine Learning}

Reproducibility is a prerequisite for evaluating both predictive results and their explanations. A published score cannot be interpreted independently of the procedure that produced it. Dataset versions, exclusion criteria, missing-value treatment, categorical encoding, feature selection, data partitioning, resampling, hyperparameter tuning, decision thresholds, and random seeds can all affect reported performance. If these elements are omitted or ambiguous, a replication may require assumptions that produce a technically reasonable workflow without reproducing the original numerical result \citep{collins_tripodai_2024, moons_probastai_2025}.

Particular care is required to prevent information from the test set from influencing model development. Imputation, scaling, feature selection, and hyperparameter tuning should be estimated using training data or internal validation procedures. Selecting the final model according to performance on the \gls{heldoutset} converts that set into part of the model-development process and can produce optimistic estimates of generalisation. In replication research, it is therefore useful to distinguish two objectives: reconstructing the procedure reported by the source paper and establishing a corrected analytical configuration for subsequent evaluation \citep{moons_probastai_2025}.

Reproducibility also affects \gls{xai}. Explanations depend on the fitted model, the feature representation, the background or reference data, the sampled observations, and method-specific settings. Two implementations that appear to use the same explainer may produce different outputs if any of these components differ. Recording feature order, model versions, random seeds, library versions, exported matrices, and fitted-model metadata is consequently part of explanation reproducibility rather than a separate software concern \citep{lombardi_explainable_2021}.

\section{Explainable Artificial Intelligence}

\gls{xai} encompasses methods intended to make the behaviour of predictive systems more accessible to human inspection. Existing taxonomies distinguish intrinsic interpretability from \gls{posthoc}, local from global scope, and model-specific from \glspl{modelagnostic} \citep{adadi_peeking_2018, barredo_arrieta_explainable_2020, linardatos_explainable_2020}. These distinctions determine what question an explanation can answer.

\Glspl{globalexplanation} summarise model behaviour across a collection of observations. They may identify variables that are influential for the fitted model overall, but aggregation can obscure heterogeneous or interaction-dependent effects. \Glspl{localexplanation} instead describe the factors contributing to an individual prediction or the model's behaviour within a small neighbourhood. Their case-specific nature can support detailed review, but a local explanation should not be generalised to the wider population without additional analysis \citep{adadi_peeking_2018, barredo_arrieta_explainable_2020, linardatos_explainable_2020, ribeiro_why_2016}.

Model-specific methods use properties of a particular estimator, such as regression coefficients or tree-based feature importance. \Glspl{modelagnostic} interact with a fitted model through its inputs and predictions and can therefore be applied across multiple model families. This flexibility facilitates standardised analysis, although these methods introduce their own assumptions, approximations, and computational costs \citep{adadi_peeking_2018, barredo_arrieta_explainable_2020, linardatos_explainable_2020, vilone_classification_2021}.

\section{Global Feature-Importance Methods}

\subsection{Native Model Importance}

Some fitted models expose a direct importance representation. For a linear classifier or logistic-regression model, the coefficient sign indicates the direction of association on the model's linear-predictor scale, while coefficient magnitude depends on feature scaling, coding, and regularisation. For tree ensembles, common native measures aggregate reductions in an impurity or loss criterion \citep{breiman_random_2001}. These quantities are specific to the estimator and should not be interpreted as universal measures of feature importance or clinical relevance \citep{linardatos_explainable_2020, vilone_classification_2021}.

\Gls{nativeimportance} is useful as a model-specific reference because it is derived directly from the parameters or structures of the fitted estimator. It is not available in a comparable form for every model family. Coefficients, split-based importance, and other native quantities have different definitions and therefore cannot be compared directly using their raw magnitudes \citep{linardatos_explainable_2020, vilone_classification_2021, fisher_models_2019}.

\subsection{Permutation Importance}

\Gls{permutationimportance} estimates the reduction in predictive performance caused by disrupting one feature while holding the fitted model fixed. It is model-agnostic and connects importance to a selected scoring metric. A feature receives greater importance when permuting its observed values produces a larger deterioration in performance \citep{fisher_models_2019}.

The method has several limitations. Correlated variables may substitute for one another, reducing or redistributing their apparent importance when considered separately. Permutation can also break observed dependencies and create feature combinations that are uncommon or implausible in the original data \citep{strobl_conditional_2008, fisher_models_2019}. Results depend on the evaluation sample, scoring metric, and random permutations. Negative values can occur when permutation produces an apparent improvement in the selected score and should not automatically be interpreted as evidence of a protective clinical effect \citep{fisher_models_2019}.

\subsection{\gls{shap}}

\gls{shap} produces \gls{featureattribution} values using concepts derived from Shapley values in cooperative game theory \citep{NIPS2017_8a20a862}. For an individual observation, the contributions relate the model prediction to an explainer-specific expected value. Aggregating absolute \gls{shap} values across observations provides a global summary of attribution magnitude, while signed values preserve the direction of contributions on the explainer's output scale \citep{NIPS2017_8a20a862, poncebobadilla_practical_2024}.

\gls{shap} provides a common attribution framework across several model classes, but its interpretation depends on the selected explainer, background distribution, assumptions about feature dependence, output scale, and implementation. Theoretical properties such as local accuracy and consistency apply under their stated definitions, but they do not imply that empirical \gls{shap} rankings are invariant across samples, retrained models, or alternative background data \citep{NIPS2017_8a20a862, poncebobadilla_practical_2024}. \gls{shap} explanations are therefore analysed as model-dependent attribution results rather than as ground-truth clinical effects \citep{vilone_classification_2021}.

\section{Local Explanations and Representative Cases}

\gls{lime} constructs a local \gls{surrogatemodel} by perturbing an observation, obtaining predictions from the original model, weighting perturbed samples according to proximity, and fitting an interpretable approximation in that neighbourhood \citep{ribeiro_why_2016}. The resulting weights describe the surrogate's local approximation rather than the original model globally. They depend on the perturbation process, discretisation, kernel, feature representation, and random seed \citep{ribeiro_why_2016, linardatos_explainable_2020, vilone_classification_2021}.

In clinical prediction research, local explanations are commonly illustrated using a small number of cases \citep{tjoa_survey_2021, yang_unbox_2021}. Arbitrary case selection can, however, emphasise convenient examples and conceal model errors. A more systematic descriptive strategy is to distinguish true-positive, true-negative, false-positive, false-negative, and decision-boundary cases. These categories connect explanation analysis to prediction outcomes and expose both successful and unsuccessful model behaviour \citep{mohseni_multidisciplinary_2021, canha_functionally-grounded_2025}.

\Gls{representativecase} analysis remains limited. One selected observation does not characterise every member of its outcome category, and a \gls{lime} rule explains the fitted local surrogate rather than establishing clinical causality. The approach is therefore most defensible as a transparent case-study procedure that complements, rather than replaces, global analysis \citep{ribeiro_why_2016, vilone_classification_2021, mohseni_multidisciplinary_2021}.

\section{Agreement Between Explanation Methods}

Different importance methods need not identify the same features because they answer different questions \citep{linardatos_explainable_2020, vilone_classification_2021}. Native importance reflects estimator-specific parameters or structures. Permutation importance measures model reliance through the deterioration of a selected performance metric after a feature is disrupted \citep{fisher_models_2019}. \gls{shap} distributes the difference between a prediction and an explainer-specific expected value among the features according to its attribution framework \citep{NIPS2017_8a20a862}. Because these methods quantify different aspects of model behaviour, disagreement is not necessarily evidence that one method has failed \citep{vilone_classification_2021}.

\Gls{rankagreement} provides a scale-independent way to examine whether methods prioritise features similarly. Spearman correlation assesses monotonic agreement between rankings, provided features are aligned by name and tied importance values receive appropriate average ranks. Top-$k$ overlap provides a complementary and more readily interpretable summary of whether the most prominent features are shared. Both measures are descriptive: high agreement does not establish clinical correctness, while low agreement may reflect correlation, interactions, estimator structure, sampling variability, or the distinct definitions of the methods \citep{mohseni_multidisciplinary_2021, vilone_classification_2021}.

Comparisons become more difficult across datasets because their feature spaces may represent different diseases, populations, coding systems, measurement procedures, or transformed design terms \citep{moons_probastai_2025}. Consequently, cross-dataset analysis should examine framework behaviour and recurring analytical patterns rather than compare raw importance magnitudes directly. Apparent lexical matches should also be interpreted cautiously when variables have different definitions, encodings, or preprocessing histories \citep{vilone_classification_2021, mohseni_multidisciplinary_2021}.

\section{Stability of Explanations}

\Gls{stability} refers broadly to the extent to which explanation results persist under a defined source of variation. The term has no single universal operational definition. Stability can be studied under perturbations of an observation, resampling of a dataset, retraining of a model, changes in the explainer background, or changes in method-specific random seeds. These designs test different properties and should not be conflated \citep{lofstrom_meta_2022, canha_functionally-grounded_2025}.

One approach to global explanation stability is to repeat model fitting under specified sources of randomness and compare the resulting feature rankings. Rank correlations and overlap among highly ranked features can then describe the persistence of the explanation output. These measures apply only to the variation introduced by the selected design. Stability under a fixed data partition, for example, does not establish stability under alternative partitions, resampled cohorts, local perturbations, or dataset shift \citep{lofstrom_meta_2022, canha_functionally-grounded_2025}.

\Gls{bootstraprefit} also requires access to the complete preprocessing and training procedure. A saved fitted model alone cannot be used to evaluate stability after perturbing the training sample. When the original procedure cannot be reproduced, reporting bootstrap-refit stability as unavailable is more defensible than substituting a different model or training process \citep{collins_tripodai_2024, canha_functionally-grounded_2025}.

\section{\gls{xai} in Clinical Decision Support}

Clinical \gls{xai} research emphasises comprehensibility, usefulness, plausibility, and support for appropriate reliance \citep{barredo_arrieta_explainable_2020, mohseni_multidisciplinary_2021}. User studies also indicate that explanations can have mixed effects. They may help users evaluate recommendations, but they can also reinforce overreliance when presented without adequate information about system limitations \citep{vasconcelos_explanations_2023, lee_understanding_2023}. Explanation presentation should therefore be evaluated in relation to intended users, clinical tasks, workflows, and decision consequences rather than through technical properties alone \citep{panigutti_co-design_2023, vasey_decideai_2022}.

Computational explanation analysis is only one stage in the evaluation of a clinical prediction system. Agreement between attribution methods or stability across repeated fits does not establish clinical usefulness, fairness, safety, or readiness for clinical use. Those questions require additional evidence that may include external and temporal evaluation, calibration analysis, subgroup assessment, prospective studies, workflow evaluation, and participation by healthcare professionals \citep{collins_tripodai_2024, moons_probastai_2025, van_calster_calibration_2019, vasey_decideai_2022, panigutti_co-design_2023}.

The present dissertation remains at the computational model-audit stage. It uses \gls{xai} to inspect fitted models, compare explanation methods, examine representative prediction outcomes, and evaluate ranking stability under a standardised framework. It does not define a universal threshold for explanation acceptance or claim that the resulting models and explanation artefacts are ready for clinical use.

\section{Comparison with Existing \gls{xai} Frameworks}

The software contribution was compared with five established Python frameworks whose purposes partially overlap but are not identical. OmniXAI provides a broad unified interface across tabular, image, text, and time-series data and includes diverse explanation families and an interactive dashboard \citep{wang_omnixai_2022}. InterpretML combines intrinsically interpretable models with local and global black-box explanation tools \citep{nori_interpretml_2019}. DALEX centres on model-agnostic exploration, model comparison, fairness analysis, and interactive visualisation \citep{baniecki_dalex_2021}. Shapash concentrates on making \gls{shap}- and \gls{lime}-based results accessible through global and local visualisations, reports, and deployment-oriented interfaces \citep{maif_shapash_2026}. Quantus has a different primary purpose: it supplies quantitative metrics for evaluating properties of attribution explanations, including faithfulness, robustness, localisation, complexity, randomisation, and axiomatic properties \citep{hedstrom_quantus_2023}.

The proposed framework is consequently narrower in modality and task coverage than OmniXAI and narrower in interactive presentation than InterpretML, DALEX, or Shapash. It also does not replace the broader attribution-quality metric catalogue offered by Quantus. \section{Research Gap and Thesis Positioning}

The literature provides extensive taxonomies of \gls{xai} methods and a growing body of medical applications, but three practical limitations remain relevant to this dissertation. First, prediction-model studies may provide insufficient information about preprocessing, model development, and evaluation to support reliable reconstruction of the underlying workflow \citep{collins_tripodai_2024, moons_probastai_2025}. Second, applied explanation analyses may present one importance method without examining whether methods based on different definitions prioritise the same variables \citep{mohseni_multidisciplinary_2021, lofstrom_meta_2022}. Third, many explanation evaluations remain limited to particular datasets, model families, or forms of explanation, restricting evidence about software transferability and analytical generalisability \citep{lofstrom_meta_2022, canha_functionally-grounded_2025}.

This dissertation addresses these limitations through a replication-and-extension design. It reconstructs five published workflows, establishes corrected final analytical configurations for the four held-out classification studies, retains the original full-cohort inferential design for \gls{hbaone}, applies a common set of global and local explanation procedures, evaluates within-model rank agreement, examines representative correct and incorrect cases, assesses bootstrap-refit feature stability where retraining is available, and synthesises framework behaviour across datasets.


The contribution is a reproducible empirical framework for studying how established \gls{xai} methods behave across heterogeneous replicated medical-prediction tasks, together with an explicit account of the limits of the resulting comparisons. It contributes a model-audit methodology and software implementation rather than a new attribution algorithm or a clinically validated decision-support system.

\section{Chapter Summary}

Medical prediction and \gls{xai} are sensitive to analytical design. Establishing how a predictive workflow was implemented and reproduced is therefore an important prerequisite for interpreting its explanations. Native importance, permutation importance, \gls{shap}, and \gls{lime} provide complementary but non-equivalent views of model behaviour. Rank agreement, representative-case analysis, and bootstrap-refit stability make the relationships among these outputs more explicit, provided that each result is interpreted according to its operational definition.

The next chapter describes the replication of the five studies, the selection of the final analytical configurations, the implementation of the standardised \gls{xai} framework, and the procedures used to synthesise dataset-level outputs in the cross-dataset analysis.

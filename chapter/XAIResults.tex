\chapter{Explainable Artificial Intelligence Results}
\label{chap:xai_results}

\section{Chapter Overview}

This chapter reports the outputs produced by the standardized XAI framework for the five final analytical configurations. The analysis covers global feature importance, agreement among global methods, repeated-fit stability, representative local explanations, cross-dataset and model-family patterns, and the demonstrated capabilities and limitations of the framework.

The results were generated from the evaluated configurations described in Chapter~\ref{chap:validation_results}. Native importance, permutation importance, and mean absolute SHAP values have different definitions and scales. Their raw magnitudes are therefore interpreted only within method and dataset. Comparisons among methods use feature ranks and top-five membership. Local LIME analysis uses rule presence and signed local weights; it does not treat LIME weights as directly comparable with global importance magnitudes.

For Heart, Stroke, Diabetes, and CKD, explanations refer to models evaluated on held-out test partitions. The HbA1c explanations are descriptive and in-sample because the replicated source analysis fitted an inferential model to the eligible cohort. The HbA1c terms also represent an encoded Patsy design matrix containing main effects and interactions rather than only raw clinical variables.

\section{Global XAI Results by Dataset}

The combined importance plots display five features or design terms per dataset. Each method's values are divided by its own maximum, and the five displayed terms are selected by their mean normalized importance across the three methods. This display selection is distinct from each method's own top-five set. Normalization facilitates visual comparison of relative importance but does not make raw effect magnitudes comparable. Full-ranking agreement is evaluated separately, using all aligned features. Negative permutation values are retained in the plots: they indicate an observed improvement in the scoring measure after permutation, not a negative clinical effect.

\subsection{Heart Disease}

For the heart logistic-regression model, \emph{thal} was ranked first by native coefficient magnitude, permutation importance, and SHAP. Native importance and SHAP also assigned high ranks to chest-pain type (\emph{cp}), exercise-induced angina (\emph{exang}), and maximum heart rate (\emph{thalach}). Permutation importance differed by placing the number of major vessels (\emph{ca}) second and sex third.

Three features---\emph{thal}, \emph{cp}, and \emph{thalach}---appeared in the top five of all three methods. The result indicates a shared core accompanied by method-specific ordering. In particular, \emph{slope} was fifth under SHAP but was not among the permutation top five, whereas sex was third under permutation but lower under native and SHAP rankings.

\subsection{HbA1c and Hospital Readmission}

The HbA1c model exhibited the clearest separation between estimator-specific coefficients and model-agnostic importance. Native coefficient magnitude was dominated by interactions between medical specialty and the youngest age group. The largest coefficient magnitude corresponded to the interaction between surgery specialty and age 30 years or younger. In contrast, permutation importance and SHAP both ranked time in hospital first, followed by missing or unknown specialty and the non-home-discharge indicator.

Permutation and SHAP shared four of their top five terms. Native importance shared none of its top five with either model-agnostic top-five set. This is not necessarily contradictory. Native ranking uses absolute coefficient magnitude, which can emphasize large coefficients for sparse encoded categories and interactions. Permutation importance measures degradation in ROC AUC when a design column is disrupted, while aggregated SHAP measures average attribution magnitude across observations. A large coefficient attached to a rare design term can consequently have limited population-level permutation or SHAP importance.

\section{Agreement Between Global XAI Methods}

Spearman rank correlation was calculated after aligning features by name and assigning average ranks to tied importance values. Table~\ref{tab:rank_agreement} reports all pairwise results.

Heart and CKD showed near-perfect agreement between native and SHAP rankings but only moderate agreement with permutation importance. Stroke followed the same general pattern, although its native--SHAP correlation was lower. Diabetes produced identical ranks across all methods. HbA1c reversed the pattern: SHAP and permutation were strongly correlated ($\rho=0.883$), while native and SHAP were almost uncorrelated ($\rho=0.047$).


\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/results/method_agreement.pdf}
    \caption{Spearman rank agreement across all aligned features, with average ranks for ties. The comparison uses 13, 7, 5, 24, and 90 features for Heart, Stroke, Diabetes, CKD, and HbA1c, respectively.}

These findings answer RQ2 by showing that agreement was neither universal nor random. Native and SHAP rankings were particularly similar for the two scikit-learn model types represented by Heart, Stroke, Diabetes, and CKD, whereas permutation rankings diverged more often. For the interaction-rich statsmodels model, the two population-oriented model-agnostic summaries agreed with one another but not with absolute coefficient magnitude. Method agreement must therefore be interpreted in relation to model structure and the quantity measured by each explanation method.

For HbA1c, absolute logistic-regression coefficients also depend on the scale and coding of each design term. Sparse categories and interactions can receive large coefficients without large average prediction contributions. The weak Native--SHAP correlation therefore does not, on its own, indicate an implementation inconsistency. Permutation of individual expanded design columns should likewise be interpreted at the encoded-term level, rather than as a clinical intervention.

Heart and Diabetes were highly stable, with mean rank correlations above 0.93. In Heart, \emph{thal} and \emph{ca} retained mean ranks of 1.0 and 2.0 with zero rank standard deviation. CKD was substantially less stable. Specific gravity remained first, and serum creatinine had a mean rank of 2.5, but many remaining permutation importances were close to or equal to zero. Repeated-fit stability was unavailable for HbA1c because the fitted bundle did not contain a retraining callback for the full inferential workflow. No substitute model or alternative stability definition was introduced. RQ4 is therefore answered for four datasets only: global rankings were highly stable for Heart and Diabetes, moderately stable for Stroke, and weakly stable beyond the leading variables for CKD.

\subsection{Illustrative LIME Results}

The strongest LIME rule for each representative case reflected local rather than population-level behaviour. In Heart, chest-pain type was the strongest rule in all five cases. Its contribution changed direction according to the discretized interval: lower chest-pain coding supported the negative class, whereas an intermediate interval supported the positive class. This rule was present both in correct predictions and errors, demonstrating that feature presence alone does not distinguish successful from unsuccessful reasoning.

In Stroke, age dominated the TP, TN, FP, and FN explanations. Age above the upper standardized interval strongly supported the positive class for both the TP and the high-confidence FP, while low age strongly supported the negative class for both the TN and FN. The borderline explanation was instead led by the BMI-missingness rule. The FP probability was 0.986 and the FN probability was 0.024, showing that prediction errors were not confined to uncertain observations.

In Diabetes, high glucose was the strongest positive rule for both the TP and FP, while low glucose supported the TN. The FN was led by a low-BMI rule, and the borderline prediction by high BMI. In CKD, serum creatinine led the TP and FP explanations, while packed cell volume and haemoglobin led the TN and FN explanations, respectively. These examples again show that similar local rules can occur in correct and incorrect predictions.

All three available HbA1c cases were led by the same absent interaction indicator involving ``Other'' specialty and age 30 years or younger. LIME weights were much larger numerically than in the other datasets, but this does not imply stronger explanations because LIME magnitude depends on the design representation, perturbation distribution, and local surrogate. The encoded interaction rule is also less directly interpretable than a raw clinical measurement.

\section{Cross-Dataset and Model-Family Comparison}

Together with the held-out results in Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap_uncertainty}, the explanation analyses distinguish predictive performance from explanation reliability. Heart combines strong discrimination with stable rankings, although agreement involving permutation is only moderate. Stroke combines high recall with low precision and lower stability than Heart. Diabetes has moderate predictive performance but identical global rankings and high retraining stability. CKD has near-perfect held-out performance yet the lowest rank stability. Thus, high predictive accuracy does not necessarily imply stable feature explanations.

The two random forests illustrate why these differences cannot be attributed to model family alone: Diabetes is highly stable, whereas CKD is substantially less stable. Feature count, near-zero importances, predictor dependence, preprocessing, and importance definitions can affect the observed rankings. HbA1c adds the complications of an interaction-rich representation, in-sample evaluation, low global--local feature overlap, and unavailable retraining stability.

Dataset-level results were grouped descriptively into linear classification, tree ensemble, and statsmodels logistic-regression families. Table~\ref{tab:model_family_results} reports the resulting means.

The valid cross-dataset conclusion is therefore about demonstrated framework behaviour, not superiority of one model family.

\section{Framework Robustness and Generalizability}

The framework successfully generated native importance, permutation importance, SHAP, representative cases, and LIME outputs for all five final configurations. It handled scikit-learn linear classifiers, scikit-learn random forests, DataFrame inputs, and the tested statsmodels logistic-regression wrapper with a Patsy design matrix. The complete automated framework test run comprised 47 tests, all of which passed (zero failures). Coverage included representative-case positional indexing and duplicate handling, native coefficient magnitudes and signs, statsmodels constant exclusion, optional native importance, CSV exports, fitted-bundle round-tripping, feature alignment and tied ranks, reproducibility comparisons, metric calculation, pipeline execution, model selection, and command-line validation and safeguards. Bootstrap-specific checks covered specificity, perfect predictions, reproducible intervals, single-class rejection, and LaTeX export structure. One warning arose in the deliberate single-class-input rejection test; it did not correspond to a failed test. These are results of the recorded framework test run, rather than evidence of universal compatibility or clinical validation.

Robustness was supported by successful execution across these heterogeneous configurations, preservation of feature order, export validation, automated tests, saved-bundle reruns, and tolerance-aware result comparison. Generalizability remains bounded by the implemented evidence. The framework has been demonstrated for binary classification only; representative cases assume a binary threshold; saved bundles cannot recreate stability without a retraining callback; local cases are descriptive selections; and raw importance magnitudes cannot be compared across methods.

The HbA1c experiment both broadened and exposed the boundary of generalizability. It demonstrated compatibility with a statsmodels result wrapper and 91-column interaction design matrix (90 non-intercept terms), but its encoded terms were less directly interpretable, computation was substantially more expensive, no held-out predictive design was available, and repeated-fit stability could not be reproduced from the bundle.

RQ5 is therefore answered conditionally. The execution and export architecture generalized across the five tested configurations, and the four principal explanation outputs were produced consistently. Scientific interpretation did not generalize without qualification: feature semantics, evaluation design, model family, class distribution, and retraining availability continued to determine what could be concluded.

\section{Chapter Summary}

The global results identified dataset-specific areas of consensus but no universal pattern of method agreement. Native and SHAP rankings were closely aligned for Heart, Stroke, Diabetes, and CKD, whereas HbA1c showed strong agreement between SHAP and permutation but almost none between native importance and SHAP. Repeated-fit stability was high for Heart and Diabetes, moderate for Stroke, and weak for CKD beyond its leading variables.

Representative-case analysis showed that the same influential rule could occur in correct and incorrect predictions. High global--local feature presence did not establish agreement in direction or correctness, and the HbA1c design terms complicated local interpretation. Cross-dataset aggregation was useful for evaluating framework coverage but insufficient for causal claims about model families.

Taken together, the findings show that explanation method, model structure, feature representation, and retraining variability materially affect the account of model behaviour. The next chapter discusses these results in relation to the research questions, the source studies, and the wider XAI literature.

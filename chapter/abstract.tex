\paragraph{}
The use of machine learning in medical prediction creates two related challenges: published predictive workflows may be difficult to reproduce, and post-hoc explanations may vary according to the model, data representation, and explanation method. This dissertation developed and evaluated a reproducible Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) framework through the replication and extension of five medical-data studies concerning heart disease, stroke, diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and HbA1c-associated hospital readmission.

The source workflows were reconstructed in dataset-specific notebooks. Paper-style replication was distinguished from corrected analytical branches whenever preprocessing order, information leakage, class imbalance, or evaluation design limited the validity of direct imitation. Four final models were evaluated on held-out test data; the HbA1c study retained its original full-cohort inferential design. A common framework then generated native model importance where supported, permutation importance, SHAP, representative-case LIME explanations, rank-agreement measures, and repeated-fit stability results when retraining was available.

Global-method agreement was strongly dataset- and representation-dependent. Native importance and SHAP were closely aligned for Heart, Stroke, Diabetes, and CKD, whereas permutation rankings diverged more often. In the interaction-rich HbA1c model, SHAP and permutation importance agreed strongly, while absolute coefficient magnitude showed almost no agreement with SHAP. Repeated-fit rankings were highly stable for Heart and Diabetes, moderately stable for Stroke, and weakly stable for CKD beyond its leading features. Representative local explanations showed that correct and incorrect predictions frequently shared the same strongest LIME rules, including confident Stroke errors. Consequently, coherent explanations did not independently establish prediction correctness.

The framework executed successfully across linear classifiers, tree ensembles, and the tested statsmodels logistic-regression model, supported by dependency locks, provenance metadata, saved bundles, command-line reruns, comparison reports, and automated tests. Its generalisability remains bounded to the tested binary-classification interfaces. No prospective, causal, clinician-centred, or clinical-utility validation was performed. The principal conclusion is that XAI should be treated as a reproducible, multi-method model-audit process rather than as an automatic certificate of clinical trust.
